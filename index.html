Blog Option 2: The Gender Pain Gap and Patient Dismissal in Women’s Health

My phenomenon is “The Gender Pain Gap” and Patient Dismissal in Women’s Health. To my current understanding, the “Gender Pain Gap” is the tendency of healthcare workers to ignore women’s physical/chronic pain and psychological symptoms. When these symptoms are presented, they are oftentimes attributed to general anxiety disorders, emotional distress, and depression, when in reality they require serious medical treatment.

One of the strongest patterns I have observed is the dismissal of calls for help from women struggling with health disorders. In “The Yellow Wallpaper,” the woman consistently tells her husband that her condition does not seem to be improving and that she is starting to feel increasingly isolated because of the “treatment” she is receiving. John, her husband, brushes this off almost instantly when she informs him. I felt that the same pattern was repeating itself in the earlier ads we saw in the ad collection. Although the ads are advertising a drug to help with anxiety and depression, they don’t give a name to the disorder, which was most likely postpartum depression or even psychosis.

This pattern of constant dismissal of calls suggests that there is a definite research opportunity and phenomenon concerning the “Gender Pain Gap” and Patient Dismissal in Women’s Health. Although I believe I have found a strong pattern, I could be assuming there is a lack of concern when it comes to women’s health, especially when the ads come into question. I did question myself there because the ads are indeed selling a drug meant to help mental disorders specifically for women, but I believe I could build on my pattern.

The three popular culture artifacts I have selected are a TikTok trend that revolves around “Late Diagnosed Female ADHD and Autism” in 2020, 1960s “Psychologized Housewife” ads, and a podcast episode that centers around physician dismissal of women’s physical pain titled “The Medical Gaslighting of Women, It’s Not Just in Your Head!” from the Advancing Women Podcast.

I believe that the phenomenon I have chosen should give me a lot of material to work with, and I am excited to do more research on the topic. While patient dismissal in women’s health could be blamed individually on close-minded physicians, popular culture artifacts such as The Bell Jar show that this is not just a one-time instance; it is a true bias that is rooted in the framework of women’s healthcare.
